A maze and an empty space

A perfect maze has exactly one way through. Cut an empty room out of its middle and watch the one way become many. The room's size is the only parameter.

Three things to look for. At zero the count of routes is 1: that is what "perfect" means for a maze. As the room grows, the route gets shorter in sudden steps, each time the room first reaches a corridor the route needed. Then the steps stop: past some size the room no longer helps you cross, but the count of equally short routes keeps climbing, because inside the room every way of weaving east and south is as good as every other. An empty space makes a maze easier to choose in long after it has stopped making it easier to get through.

What is counted, exactly

The maze is a 31 × 21 grid carved by seeded depth-first search, entrance at the top-left cell, exit at the bottom-right. The empty space is a square of side k centred in the grid with every wall inside it removed. A breadth-first search from the entrance gives the shortest distance to every cell; the number of shortest routes is counted by adding, for each cell, the counts of its neighbours one step closer. A cell is on some shortest route when its distance from the entrance plus its distance from the exit equals the route length. The drawn route prefers east and south moves on ties, so it is one definite route among the counted ones.
