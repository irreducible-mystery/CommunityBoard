Tetralemma, many qubits

Sequel to six and seven. Nothing here leaves the page.

Seven showed that the perfect neither cannot be built: some group of three always keeps something of its own. From eight qubits on, the counting bounds rule it out too. This page stops asking for perfection and asks what you get for free. Take n qubits in a state chosen at random, with no design at all, and look at a group of k of them. How close is that group to having nothing of its own?

The answer is almost perfect, almost everywhere. A small group in a large random state is maximally mixed to within a rounding error. The shortfall roughly quadruples with each qubit added to the group. Only at half does a definite gap remain, and for an even number of qubits it is the same at every size: about 0.72 bits, or 1/(2 ln 2). The perfection that design could not reach at seven, chance comes within a hair of, except at half.

1. The shortfall, group by group

S(k) is the entropy of the first k qubits, in bits; k is the most it can be. The column Page is the average shortfall over all random states, from Don Page's exact formula (1993): for a group of dimension m against a rest of dimension N ≥ m, the mean entropy is Σj=N+1mN 1/j − (m−1)/(2N) nats. One drawn state lands close to the average, closer as n grows, because random states concentrate. The last column is the ratio to the step before. It approaches 4, because the mean shortfall is very nearly (m − 1/m)/(2N ln 2) bits, about 22k−n/(2 ln 2). At half, m = N, and the same expression gives 1/(2 ln 2). Each qubit moved from the rest to the group doubles the group's dimension and halves the rest's, and the shortfall goes as their ratio. For the smallest groups, one draw can sit far from the average in relative terms; the shortfall is tiny either way, and the ratio column settles once the numbers are large enough to measure. Twenty qubits is the most a page can hold: 220 complex amplitudes, 16 MB. The half cut at twenty is a 1024×1024 matrix and takes a few seconds.

2. The constant at half

At half, the group and the rest have the same dimension. Page's formula then tends to n/2 − 1/(2 ln 2) as n grows, so the shortfall stops shrinking and settles at 0.7213 bits. This is how much a random state falls short of perfect where perfection matters most. Six qubits can close it, and the page before showed that seven cannot. A random state never closes it at any size.

For odd n, "half" means the cut of ⌊n/2⌋ against ⌈n/2⌉. The rest is then twice as big, and the gap is about half as large (0.36). That is the same quadrupling rule seen from the other side.

3. Every group of three

Section 1 looks at one group of each size, the first k. A perfect state needs every group to be mixed. Here every group of three in the current state is checked, for states of up to 14 qubits.

At seven no state can make all 35 triples exactly 3. At twelve, a random state with no design makes all 220 of them 2.98 or more. The impossibility at seven is about exact zeros. Chance never produces exact zeros, but at size it produces small numbers everywhere.

4. The cat and the crowd

Two states of the same size, asked questions one qubit at a time ("is it?", a Z measurement, answers drawn with the seed). The cat is GHZ: all |0⟩ or all |1⟩. The relation is held by the whole, as on the three-qubit page, where neither first split in two. The crowd is a random state. After each answer the table shows the entanglement left across the middle of the qubits that remain.

The cat has exactly one bit of both, spread across every cut. The first answer removes it completely, and what is left is n−1 qubits that are each simply it is, or each it is not. The crowd has almost n/2 bits across its middle. Each answer costs it about half a bit, because the remaining state is again a random state, one qubit smaller. Fragility belongs to the relation that only the whole holds. Robustness belongs to a relation spread over everything, which no single question can find.

What this is not

Random states are not what any group of people is in, and twenty qubits are not a crowd. What the toy is exact about is a trade. Designed states reach perfection at two, three, five and six qubits, and the attempt at seven fails. Undesigned states never reach it, but they come within a hair, everywhere except at half, and the gap there is a fixed number. Asked one question, the designed-looking cat loses everything and the crowd loses half a bit. The fourth corner, neither, turns out to be typical rather than rare in large systems, and the exact version of it is what becomes impossible. The third page in this set, a surface code, goes back to design. It puts a relation where no small accident can find it, so that it can be repaired.
