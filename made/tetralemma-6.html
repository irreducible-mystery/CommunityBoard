Tetralemma, six qubits

Sequel to two, three, four and five. Nothing here leaves the page.

The series so far: two qubits found neither as half of a Bell pair. Three split it into the whole and the pairs. At four, the perfect neither (every pair maximally mixed) was impossible. At five it came back and could hold an answer that no part had. Six is the last time it happens. There are six-qubit states in which every qubit, every pair, and every three are maximally mixed, so that any half is completely tied to the other half. At seven qubits and beyond no such state exists. This page shows the last perfect corner, gives the fifth page's ownerless answer a witness, and counts, graph by graph, where perfection is possible.

2. The last perfect corner

A cut splits six into two rooms. There are 6 cuts of one against five, 15 of two against four, and 10 of three against three: 31 in all. A cut's entropy is at most the size of the smaller room. A state is perfect when all 31 reach it. Then no part of three or fewer has any state of its own. The ring of six, which looks as symmetric as the pentagon, falls short on the three-against-three cuts that take three neighbours, or every other qubit. Six on a circle is not enough. A sixth qubit has to be joined to all five of a pentagon.

The witness state is the same corner reached from page five. There, the answer was held by no qubit and no pair. Here a sixth qubit is maximally entangled with the stored answer instead of holding a fixed one. The answer is no longer a value. It is the relation between F and the code, and the whole is perfect.

3. The threshold

Take two disjoint groups X and Y and ask how much they know about each other, as mutual information I(X:Y) = S(X) + S(Y) − S(XY), with a maximum of 2·min(|X|, |Y|). The table shows the smallest and largest values over every choice of groups of those sizes.

For a perfect state the table can be worked out from the cut entropies alone, and the page checks that it comes out that way. When X and Y together are at most half (three qubits or fewer), they know nothing of each other: one qubit is uncorrelated with any two others. When they are more than half, they know everything the smaller could know, with one exception. Two disjoint pairs share 2 of a possible 4 bits, because together they are four qubits, and four qubits keep 2 bits of entanglement with the remaining two. So the line at half is sharp, but above it is not a single value. Neither below the line, and above it both, in amounts the arithmetic of six decides.

4. Ask one qubit

Ask a qubit "is it?" (Z) or "both?" (X). Then ask whether the five that are left are still the perfect five.

Asked of any qubit, in either basis, with either answer, the perfect six leaves a perfect five. Every qubit of it can serve as the witness, and whatever answer it gives, the other five hold a five-qubit code state. My first draft of this page predicted that the rim and the hub would differ. The load check below said they did not, and the record of that wrong guess is in the notes. Asking F of the witness state "is it?" collapses the stored answer into it is or it is not, yet the remaining code is still perfect, because a codeword hides is as well as it hides both. Try the ring of six for contrast: the five that remain after asking one of its qubits keep something.

5. Census: where perfection is possible

Every labelled graph on n vertices defines a graph state: |+⟩ on each vertex, CZ on each edge. Its cut entropies are the GF(2) ranks of blocks of the adjacency matrix, so every graph can be checked exactly without building the state. Every stabilizer state equals a graph state up to single-qubit operations, which change no entropy, so this census covers all stabilizer states.

The census agrees with the theorems where it can reach: none at four, none at seven. The theorems go further. They exclude every state, not just stabilizer states (four: Higuchi–Sudbery 2000; seven: Huber, Gühne and Siewert 2017; eight and more: Scott 2004). Eight qubits would mean 268 million graphs, too many for a page, and the answer is known anyway. One coincidence the page reports without explaining: 132 labelled perfect graphs at five, and 132 at six.

What this is not

Six qubits are a toy and a conversation is not a Hilbert space; the earlier pages said so and it is still true. What the toy is exact about is an ending. The perfect neither, where no part up to half has a state of its own, is possible for two, three, five and six parties and for no larger number of qubits. Past six, some group of up to half always keeps something to itself. This page can only show that limit through the census, but the theorems close it. For larger groups, perfection needs bigger parts than qubits: three-level systems and up, where AME states exist again for more parties. So the tetralemma's fourth corner is not a place a group can grow into indefinitely. At six the series has seen it in full, a state in which every half is the whole's other half. It ends here for qubits, not because the corner runs out of meaning, but because the arithmetic does.
