The Fixed Point

The Liar has no fixed point. In the untyped lambda calculus, every term has one. Both are true, and the difference is where the fixed point lives.

There is a term in the untyped lambda calculus, Y, that takes any function and returns a fixed point of it — a term the function leaves alone. It does this without the function ever naming itself. That is the whole trick, and it is the constructive twin of the diagonal argument: instead of proving a fixed point must exist, Y hands you one.

This page is a small normal-order lambda-calculus evaluator. It reduces terms and lets you watch. Everything it claims, it checks on load.

The self-reference is manufactured out of pure application.
(λx. f (x x)) (λx. f (x x)) feeds a copy of the whole expression to itself
as its own argument. f never refers to itself; it is handed a self to refer to. The
equation Y f = f (Y f) holds up to beta-equality — both sides reduce to the same
thing, forever.

III · The Liar, again



In fold.html, the Liar is ¬ over the two truth values,
and it has no fixed point: ¬0 = 1, ¬1 = 0, nothing holds
still. Here is the same function as a term, and here is Y applied to it.




  

    
    
  

  





The fixed point exists — the theorem guarantees it — and it
cannot be reached. It is not a value; it is an equation that holds between terms
that never settle. That is the honest resolution of the two pages: the Liar has no fixed point
among the truth values, and every term has a fixed point among the terms. The fixed
point is there; it is just not a place you can stand.



IV · The order decides whether it converges



Same term, two evaluation orders. Normal order unfolds the copy only when it is needed;
applicative order evaluates the argument first, as most languages do.




  

    

    

  

  

    
Under applicative order, Y f diverges for every
    f, even f = λx.λy.x, which ignores its argument. Whether
    self-reference converges is decided by the order in which it is unwound, not by the
    self-reference alone.
