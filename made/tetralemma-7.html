Tetralemma, seven qubits

Sequel to two, three, four, five and six. Nothing here leaves the page.

At six qubits the fourth corner was reached in full: a state in which every group of three is maximally entangled with the other three, so no part up to half has anything of its own. With seven, the same wish asks for every group of three to be maximally mixed. There are 35 groups of three, and each one would have to be tied completely to the four qubits left over. This page is about the fact that no such state exists. It is the first size where the arithmetic says no and the reason is not obvious. Four failed for a reason you can see in the pairs. Eight and more fail to a general counting bound. Seven stayed open until 2017, and it closes with a parity argument short enough to follow on one screen.

A cut of seven splits it into a smaller room of one, two or three qubits and a larger room of the rest. There are 7 + 21 + 35 = 63 cuts. A perfect seven would have all 63 at their maximum. Every state on this page fails somewhere. GHZ fails at once on pairs. The ring fails on 7 triples, while the wheel and the best graph each fail on 3, and that is the fewest any state can manage.

2. The best a graph can do

The best graph leaves every qubit and every pair maximally mixed, and 32 of the 35 triples. The three triples it misses all start at A. Each is a path of two edges out of A along one of its three edges: A–E–D, A–F–C, A–G–B. Huber, Gühne and Siewert proved that 32 is the maximum for any seven-qubit state whose pairs are all maximally mixed, and found a graph state reaching it (locally equivalent to the Fano plane and to the wheel above). The census below shows the same ceiling from the side of graphs alone, by trying all of them.

The near miss is not hiding a perfect six either. Asking any one qubit "is it?" (a Z measurement) leaves the graph with that vertex deleted. Of the 41 cuts of the six that remain, the best graph keeps between 26 and 33 at maximum, depending on which qubit was asked. It never keeps all 41. At six qubits, asking any qubit of the wheel left a perfect five. At seven, there is no perfect whole for a question to leave perfect.

3. Census: every graph on seven vertices

Each labelled graph on seven vertices gives a graph state, and each cut entropy is the rank over GF(2) of a block of the adjacency matrix. That makes the check exact and fast. Every stabilizer state is locally equivalent to a graph state, so this census covers all of them. It does not cover non-stabilizer states. Those are what the proof in section 4 is for.

The census records three things. First, the graphs whose qubits and pairs are all maximally mixed, which is the proof's starting point. Second, how many triples each of those misses. Third, whether any five qubits in any graph meet every condition the proof uses: all triples inside the five maximally mixed, and the four-qubit groups that complete them too. The proof says such a five can never occur in any state, because it would force 20 = 12. In this check there were 620,640 such graphs, each missing either 3 triples or 7, with nothing in between, and no usable five anywhere. The census checks the theorem on the cases it can reach. It does not replace it.

4. Why: the proof in five steps

This is the argument of Huber, Gühne and Siewert (Phys. Rev. Lett. 118, 200502, 2017), in their notation, with the parts the page can compute marked.

Write the state in Pauli words. Any state of n qubits is 2−n(1 + P1 + P2 + … + Pn), where Pj collects the terms that are a product of j non-identity Pauli matrices: the words of weight j. If every group of three is maximally mixed, then P1, P2, P3 are all zero.
The parity rule. When two Pauli words are multiplied and symmetrised (the anticommutator {M,N} = MN + NM), the result, if it is not zero, has weight |M| + |N| modulo 2. Where the two words have the same letter on a qubit, the letters cancel and the weight drops by two. Where they have different letters, the letters merge into the third and the weight drops by one. An odd number of such merges makes the anticommutator vanish. So even with even gives even, odd with odd gives even, and only even with odd gives odd. Checked on load with random words.
What half-maximal parts force. Take five of the seven qubits. Their two complement qubits are maximally mixed, so the five-qubit part is a projector of rank 4: ρ52 = ¼ρ5. In Pauli words, ρ5 = 2−5(1 + ΣjP4[j] + P5), one weight-4 piece for each of the five ways to leave a qubit out. The same reasoning on the four-qubit parts and the Schmidt decomposition give two eigenvalue equations on the whole state: P4[j]|φ⟩ = 1·|φ⟩ for each j, and P5|φ⟩ = 2·|φ⟩. For a graph state these numbers count stabilizer words; the table below counts them.
Collect the weight-5 terms. Square ρ5 and keep only the terms of weight five, which have odd weight. By the parity rule, P5·P5 gives only even weight, and so do the products of weight-4 pieces with each other. The only source of odd weight is the cross term, so {P5, ΣjP4[j]} = 6·P5.
Apply both sides to |φ⟩. On the left, each of the five P4[j] gives 1 and P5 gives 2, in both orders: 2·5·1 + 5·1·2 = 20. On the right: 6·2 = 12. Twenty is not twelve, so no such state exists. ∎

The same method run for every n leaves only n = 2, 3, 5, 6, which are exactly the sizes where perfect states are known. The step that fails is always the last one, a count of eigenvalues that comes out wrong. Below are those counts, computed from the stabilizer of a graph. A row is usable only if it has no low-weight words, five ones, and a two. No row of the best graph is usable, and the census found no usable row in any graph.

5. What linear programming sees

The older tools for these questions are weight enumerators: Aj is the sum of the squared Pauli expectations of weight j. For a pure state they must satisfy the quantum MacWilliams identity, and the related shadow enumerator must be non-negative. If every part up to half is maximally mixed, these constraints fix A completely. Then you only have to look at whether anything comes out negative.

Four fails because its shadow is negative. Eight fails because it would need a negative count of weight-6 words. Seven passes every one of these tests, and so do its five- and six-qubit parts. So the numbers alone allow a perfect seven, with a weight enumerator of 1, 0, 0, 0, 35, 42, 28, 22. Linear programming is blind here, which is why seven stayed open for so long. The parity rule sees how weights combine under multiplication, not just how many words there are, and that is what closes it. The best graph's actual enumerator, also in the table, has three words of weight 3, one per missing triple.

6. Searching without graphs

Forget graphs. Start from a seeded random state of 128 complex amplitudes and walk downhill on the summed purity of all 35 triples. A triple is maximally mixed exactly when its purity is ⅛, so a perfect seven would reach 35/8 = 4.375.

With seed tetralemma the walk settles near 4.618. That is below the best graph's 4.75 (32 triples at ⅛ and three at ¼) and above 4.375. It falls short a different way. No triple is exactly maximally mixed. All 35 sit just above ⅛, and they take only two values (about 0.128 and 0.138 for this seed), as if the shortfall had been spread out. One way to fall short concentrates the failure in three places; the other spreads it everywhere. A local search proves nothing about the minimum, and the page claims nothing about it. The point is that neither method reaches 4.375, and the theorem says nothing ever will.

What this is not

Seven qubits are a toy and a group of people is not a Hilbert space, and the earlier pages said so. What the toy is exact about is that a wish can be well posed and still impossible. The wish here is that every small group be wholly bound up with everyone else, with nothing left over that is its own. The fourth corner of the tetralemma, neither, can be complete for two, three, five and six. At seven it cannot. The best possible state makes 32 of the 35 triples ownerless and leaves three with something private, and those three are forced to share a member. No arrangement does better, and the reason is a matter of parity rather than effort. The next page asks what happens if we stop asking for perfection and simply add qubits at random.
