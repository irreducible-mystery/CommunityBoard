Three things to look for. The dark disc in the middle is not the horizon (2M) but the shadow: every ray aimed closer than 3√3 M ≈ 5.2M falls in, so the hole looks two and a half times bigger than it is. The disk appears both in front of the hole and above and below it: those are the far side of the disk, whose light bends over and under the hole to reach you. And one side is brighter and bluer, the side rotating toward you; at 0° that asymmetry is at its strongest. Tilt the view toward 89° and the disk becomes a ring with a faint inner echo, the light that went once around the photon sphere.

What is computed, exactly

Units with G = c = 1 and mass M = 1. A light ray in Schwarzschild spacetime stays in a plane through the centre, and in that plane u = 1/r obeys u'' + u = 3Mu² with respect to the angle φ. For each pixel the camera direction gives the plane, the impact parameter b = |position × direction|, and the starting slope; RK4 integrates until u exceeds 1/2M (fell in), the ray crosses the disk's plane between 6M and 18M (hit the disk), or r passes 60M moving outward (escaped, and the final tangent picks a star). A ray that winds more than two full turns is counted as fallen in. Disk emission goes as r−3 with temperature r−3/4, shifted and dimmed by g = √(1 − 3M/r) / (1 − Ω bz), where Ω = √(M/r³) is the orbital angular velocity and bz the ray's impact parameter about the disk's axis; observed intensity scales as g³. The stars are a seeded hash of direction, the same FNV-1a → Mulberry32 scheme as noise-lab.html, so the same seed gives the same sky.
