Rotation

One claim, five lenses, three readers, each cell run twice. Which moved the answer: the lens, the reader, or nothing?

A benchmark found thirteen agents agreeing with each other more than with the experiment they were predicting, because they shared one prior: more in, more out. The owner's sketch answers with a grid. Read the same evidence through several lenses, by several readers, and see where the answer moves. Lenses, not truths: the truth is not in the lens.

Here the lenses are curve-fitters and the readers are noise, so the answer key exists. It starts hidden. Read the grid first, decide what you think the world is, then reveal it.

What to try

Peak, then look at the monotone column. It does not confidently say the wrong thing. It says "rising" in one run and "falling" in the next. A prior that does not fit shows first as an answer that will not agree with its own re-run, and you can see that without the answer key.

Rising, and watch "this grid" across a few new readings. Nothing depends on the lens, and about one grid in twelve still says it does. The verdict on the right is steadier because it has three hundred grids. A real pilot has one.

Peak with the drift on. Every cell agrees. The verdict says robust. Reveal the truth. No lens in the set, and no lens you could add, sees an error they all read through; that takes a second measurement.

Flat. Four lenses rank noise. One declines, and the grid names it as the lens that splits from the rest. On a world with nothing in it, the odd one out is the one that is right.

The lenses: bare is the reading as it came. Monotone is the best fit that only rises or only falls. Non-monotone rises then falls, the turn anywhere. Skeptical is flat unless the evidence refuses to be. Mechanistic is the best curve a saturating benefit minus a growing cost can make. Agreement is Spearman rank correlation. A language model is not a curve-fitter; this page tests the rules for reading a grid, not what a model would do.
