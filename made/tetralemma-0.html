Tetralemma, zero qubits

Below two, the bottom of the series. Next: approaching infinity and the infinite cat (each in its own pull request; the links work once they are merged). Nothing here leaves the page.

Every page in this series has asked one kind of question: what does a part know? It is, it is not, both and neither describe how an answer is shared between pieces of a system. Two qubits gave the first neither, the Bell pair. Five gave the perfect hidden answer. Seven showed that no perfect state exists. This page goes the other way, down to the bottom.

1. Zero qubits: one state, no parts

n qubits have 2n amplitudes. For n = 0 that is 20 = 1: one complex number of length one. Its phase is unobservable, so there is exactly one state. It has no parts and no cuts. Nothing can be measured, and its entropy is zero. All four corners are claims about parts, so none of them has anything to describe.

The test for a perfect state (every part of at most half the qubits maximally mixed) still has to be run on something. The only part of at most half of nothing is the empty part. A one-dimensional density matrix equal to 1 is both pure and maximally mixed, so the test passes. One qubit passes for the same reason: half of one rounds down to zero. Lists of perfect states start at two because zero and one pass without testing anything. The page runs the test, and the table shows how much it actually examined.

2. Where every calculation ends

Zero qubits is not only a curiosity. It is where every finished calculation lands. Ask the qubits of a state their answers one at a time. Each question halves the list of amplitudes and keeps the half that matches. After the last question one number is left: a zero-qubit state, not normalised. Its squared length is the probability of the answers you chose. Every question you could ask has an answer of this kind.

3. One qubit: nobody to share with

One qubit is the smallest thing that can be in superposition. Every one-qubit state points somewhere on the Bloch sphere, and every pure state sits on its surface. A pure state of one qubit always has purity 1, but there is no second party to be entangled with. There is no cut, and so no neither in the sense these pages use, meaning correlations that no single part holds.


4. Approaching zero

The qubit count cannot approach zero. It is a whole number, and so is the dimension. Nothing lies between one qubit and none. What can approach zero is entanglement. Take √(1−p)|00⟩ + √p|11⟩ and let p shrink. The entanglement S(p) = −p log2p − (1−p) log2(1−p) goes to zero, but its slope log2((1−p)/p) goes to infinity. Near p = 0, S ≈ p log2(e/p), which is far more than p itself. A tiny second branch buys a large amount of entanglement relative to its weight.

S(p), in bits, against p on a logarithmic axis. The dashed line is p, so the gap between them is the entanglement beyond the branch's weight.

This is the opposite of the large-n story. There, entanglement is cheap and becomes almost perfect. Here it begins abruptly: any nonzero branch, however small, is already entangled, and per unit of weight the smallest branches buy the most.

What this is not

Zero qubits is a statement about bookkeeping, not about physics with nothing in it. The rest of the universe is still there, and in quantum field theory even the vacuum is entangled (see the infinity page). The vacuous pass of the perfect-state test is a property of how the definition is written, not a discovery. The "approaching zero" slider uses one family of states. Other families approach zero differently, but every one passes through states with an arbitrarily small second branch, and those always have infinite slope.
