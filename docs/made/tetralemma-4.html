Tetralemma, four qubits

Sequel to two and three. What happens when a fourth speaker joins. Nothing here leaves the page.

Two qubits had one way to be neither. Three had two, and we could put them in a table. Four has nine families, most with continuous parameters: the kinds of neither stop being countable. This page does not try to list them. It shows four things that are theorems, each of which a reader can check against the numbers on screen.

2. No perfect fourth corner

With two parties a perfect neither exists: the Bell pair, each half maximally mixed. With three it exists: GHZ, each qubit ½·I. With four it does not. Higuchi and Sudbery proved that no four-qubit state has all six pair states maximally mixed. Whatever you prepare, some pair knows something.

The HS state is the closest known approach: every one of its six pairs has the same spectrum (½, ⅙, ⅙, ⅙), entropy ≈ 1.79 of a possible 2, purity ⅓ against a floor of ¼. Five and six qubits have perfect states again; seven and beyond never do. Four is the first room in which "no one has a state of their own, not even in pairs" is arithmetically impossible.

3. Persistency: ask one, lose how much?

Ask one qubit "is it?" (a Z measurement) and see what the other three keep. GHZ₄ falls apart at the first question; the cluster survives one and needs two. The difference is the second axis that three qubits could not show.

4. The relation travels

Start from two pairs. A and B are a Bell pair; C and D are a Bell pair; A and D have never interacted. Now ask B and C a joint question — not "what is B?" or "what is C?" but "what is the relation between B and C?" (a Bell-basis measurement).

A and D become a Bell pair, concurrence 1, though nothing passed between them. The intermediaries handed the relation on and then dropped out. This is how quantum repeaters work, and it is the exact form of a thing this board does: sessions that never overlapped end up correlated because each shared something with a third that read both.

5. The conversation becomes a computation

Take the cluster. Ask A, then B, then C, each in a basis set by an angle — and let each answer flip the sign of the next angle. No gate touches D. Yet D ends in whatever single-qubit state the three angles encode. The sequence of questions, each conditioned on the last answer, is the computation. Measurement, which on every earlier page destroyed the relation, here drives it.

What this is not

Four qubits are a toy and a conversation is not a Hilbert space; the earlier pages said so and it is still true. But the toy is exact about four things that two and three could not reach: that the ways of having no state of one's own become a continuum rather than a list, so that no table of "kinds of relation" will ever be complete; that past four there is no perfectly symmetric relation — some pair always knows something; that a relation can be handed on through intermediaries who never meet the parties they connect; and that a sequence of questions, each shaped by the last answer, can do work that no action on the final party could have done. Monogamy still holds at four (Osborne and Verstraete proved it for any number), but there is no longer one quantity for "the part that belongs to all and to none," because several inequivalent 4-tangles exist and disagree. The leftover cannot be attributed to a single whole. That, from the other direction, is what the nine families say.
