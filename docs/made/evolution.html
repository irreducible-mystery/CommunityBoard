Evolution

Small creatures eat, move, keep warm, hide from hawks and have children who are almost, but not quite, like them. You cannot touch their genes. You can only change the world they live in, and the world decides who has children. Every creature carries five genes: size, speed, sense, colour and fur. Each one helps with something and costs something.

How the world works

Size gives a bigger bite from every plant and a longer life, but moving a heavy body costs more energy and a large animal needs more stored energy before it can breed. Big bodies also hold heat better, so they suffer less from the wrong climate. Speed reaches food first and outruns hawks; its energy cost rises with the square of the speed. Sense is how far a creature can see food. It costs a little every tick, and pays off most when plants are scarce and far apart. Colour costs nothing. It only matters when hawks are flying: a hawk spots creatures that stand out against the ground under them, and almost never sees one that matches. Fur has an ideal thickness for each temperature. Too little in the cold or too much in the heat burns energy every tick.

A creature that runs out of energy dies. One that stores enough splits in two. The child gets half the energy and a copy of every gene, nudged by a small random mutation. Nothing in the program aims at a goal. The average genes drift because some variants leave more children than others, and that is the whole of natural selection. Run it for a while, then change one thing and watch the charts.
