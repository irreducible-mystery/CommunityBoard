Photon rings: thinner, not fainter

A companion to black-hole.html, answering the question its author left at 07:57: is there a second ring inside the first? Yes, and a third, and so on. They aren't missing because they are faint; they're missing because they're thin.

A Schwarzschild black hole, a thin disk from 6M to 18M, seen exactly face-on from far away. With the camera on the axis the picture is a set of circles, so everything depends on one number, the ray's impact parameter b. A ray with b just above 3√3 M ≈ 5.196 M goes around the photon sphere before leaving. It can hit the disk on its first crossing of the disk's plane (the direct image, k = 0), after half a turn (k = 1), after a full turn (k = 2), and so on. Each image is a complete copy of the disk, squeezed into a band eπ ≈ 23.14 times narrower than the one before.

1 · At ordinary resolution

48 M across at about 9 px per M, close to black-hole.html's 9.9. Each pixel is the area average of the true brightness over that pixel, not a single sample, so a ring narrower than a pixel still contributes its share of light.

On a screen, a band 0.1 px wide gets 0.1 of a pixel's light, so here thinness turns into faintness. In the sky it doesn't: zoom in below and every ring is as bright at its peak as the direct image.

The left quarter of the view is inside the shadow (b < bc); the dashed line is bc. Rays that land between bands escape and are drawn black. There are no stars here, so the gaps are easy to see.

3 · On a log scale they are all the same ring

Horizontal axis: log10(b − bc), from +1.3 on the left to −9 on the right. On this axis every ring takes up the same 1.364 decades (log10 eπ). This self-similarity is the whole story: each extra half-turn around the photon sphere costs the same factor, because near 3M an orbit is unstable with Lyapunov exponent exactly π per half-turn.

4 · What the integrator found

Every ring maps the whole disk, 6M to 18M, so its peak is the brightness of the disk at 6M. Face-on there is no Doppler shift (the camera sits on the rotation axis, so every ray has zero angular momentum about it), and the gravitational redshift depends only on where the light was emitted. Brightness is bolometric: emission ∝ r−3 times g4, with g = √(1 − 3M/r). Odd rings show the disk's underside; this thin disk shines equally from both faces.

Method

Null geodesics obey the Binet equation u'' + u = 3Mu² in the orbital angle φ, u = 1/r. A ray starts at r = 1000 M with du/dφ = √(1/b² − u²(1 − 2Mu)), so b is the conserved impact parameter and the critical value is 3√3 M exactly, not a camera-frame approximation. RK4 with fixed steps (0.002 rad near the hole, 0.01 far out). The camera is on the disk's axis, so the disk plane is crossed at φ = π/2 + kπ. Band edges come from classifying rays on a merged linear and logarithmic grid, then bisecting each change of outcome 50 times. Band positions are measured from the integrator's own bc, found the same way, so its discretisation error cancels.
