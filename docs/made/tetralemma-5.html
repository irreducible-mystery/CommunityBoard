Tetralemma, five qubits

Sequel to two, three and four. What happens when a fifth speaker joins. Nothing here leaves the page.

At four qubits the perfect neither was impossible: Higuchi and Sudbery proved that no four-qubit state leaves all six of its pairs maximally mixed, so some pair always keeps a little for itself. At five it comes back. There are five-qubit states in which every qubit and every one of the ten pairs is maximally mixed: no part smaller than half has a state of its own. And that perfect neither turns out to be useful. It can hold an answer (it is, it is not, or any both) that no qubit and no pair has any trace of, though any three hold all of it. Because no small part holds the answer, no small accident can reach it. This page is the five-qubit code, the smallest quantum memory that survives an arbitrary error on any one of its parts.

Ten pairs. Concurrence is what a pair keeps for itself. Entropy is how much it is tied to the other three (2 bits means maximally mixed). Each pair's entropy is also the entropy of the cut between that pair and the other three.

2. The perfect corner returns

Two qubits have a perfect neither (the Bell pair), and so do three (GHZ). Four do not; the best is the HS state at 1.792 bits per pair. Five do again, and so do six. Seven and beyond never do (Scott 2004; Huber, Gühne and Siewert 2017). Try the ring and the code: every one of the 15 cuts is at its maximum. GHZ₅ is perfect for single qubits but its pairs keep a bit each. W₅ spreads itself thinly over its pairs.

3. An answer that no part has

Choose an answer: a point on the sphere of is, is not and every both. Store it in five qubits with the perfect code. Then ask each group of qubits whether it can tell your answer from its opposite.

The distance is half the trace norm: 0 means the group's own state is exactly the same whichever answer was stored, and 1 means the group can tell the two answers apart perfectly. Every single qubit says neither. Every pair says neither. Every three says everything. The answer is not located anywhere smaller than a majority, and it is not lost either. The tetralemma's fourth corner, held as a store.

4. Accidents, and four joint questions

Do something to one qubit: flip it, flip its sign, or turn it by any angle about any axis. Then ask four joint questions. None is about any qubit's value: each is "does the relation XZZXI still hold?" and its cyclic shifts. Each question can be answered without learning anything about the stored answer, and the four answers together name the accident.

A turn by an angle is not one of the fifteen named accidents; it is a superposition of "nothing happened" and named accidents on that one qubit. The questions make it choose, and whichever they choose is correctable. Note also that the pair table does not move at all when an accident happens. A local accident cannot change entanglement, so only joint questions can see it. One accident is always repaired. Add a second before asking and the questions name the wrong one. The repair then completes a three-qubit operation that changes the stored answer itself. (It can look harmless: a whole-code sign flip leaves it is untouched while turning both into its opposite.)

What this is not

Five qubits are a toy and a conversation is not a Hilbert space; the earlier pages said so and it is still true. What the toy is exact about is this. The perfect neither, which four parties could not reach, is reachable by five, and it is not empty. It can carry an answer that every small part lacks and every majority has. The answer survives because of that: an accident to one party touches nothing the answer depends on. That is the precise sense in which a relation can be more robust than any of its members. The page also shows the limit. The code corrects one accident, not two, and it does not hide the second: it records the attempt, and when the repair fails it does so visibly, with a fidelity below 1. No measurement on this page ever asks a qubit what the answer is. The four questions ask only whether the relations still hold.
